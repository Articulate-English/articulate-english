B2 Exam Speaking Practice

Crime, Law & Justice

Practice Part 1 interview, Part 2 photo comparison, Part 3 collaborative task, and extra homework.

Part 1 — Interview

Part 1 — Interview

Answer these questions. Spend about 20 seconds on each.

1. How safe do you feel in the area where you live?
I feel remarkably safe where I live. It is a quiet neighbourhood with little traffic, and I am not worried about walking home after dark.
2. To what extent do you think poverty is connected to crime?
I believe the connection is significant. When people cannot find work or afford basic necessities, some of them turn to theft simply to survive.
3. Do you follow news about court cases or crime stories?
Only occasionally. I find some trials fascinating, but I try not to read too much because the details can be genuinely distressing.
4. How has online crime changed in recent years?
It has evolved considerably. Fraud has moved almost entirely online, and criminals now use convincing fake websites and messages to trick people.
5. What do you think makes a punishment fair?
I think a fair punishment should fit the crime and give the person a chance to change. Sending someone to prison for a minor offence seems disproportionately harsh.
Useful phrases:
To a significant extent...I am inclined to think...It has evolved considerably.That is a fair point, but...On balance...It depends on the circumstances.
Part 2 — Long Turn

Part 2 — Photo Comparison

Talk for 1 minute. Compare the photos and answer the question.

What message about justice do these two photos convey?
In the first photo, a police officer is handcuffing someone outdoors, so it shows the moment a suspect is detained by the authorities. In the second photo, a judge in robes and a wig is sitting at the bench in a courtroom, holding a gavel, which represents the trial and the final decision. Together they convey that justice has two distinct stages: first the police enforce the law, and then an independent court decides what happens. I find the second image particularly striking, because the courtroom suggests that punishment should be considered carefully rather than imposed immediately.
Part 3 — Discussion

Part 3 — Collaborative Task

Discuss with your partner for 2–3 minutes, then decide.

A town wants to reduce crime among young people. Discuss the ideas and decide which two would be most effective.
  • More police officers on the streets
  • Free sports and music clubs in the evenings
  • Harsher prison sentences for young offenders
  • Mentoring programmes with local adults
  • Better street lighting and security cameras
A: I think more police on the streets would deter crime, because people are less likely to break the law if they might be caught.
B: That is a reasonable argument, but it does not deal with the causes of crime. Free sports and music clubs would give young people something rewarding to do in the evenings.
A: I agree, and mentoring programmes could be even more effective, since a trusted adult can genuinely change how a teenager sees their future.
B: Harsher sentences worry me. They sound tough, yet they often make reoffending worse rather than better.
A: So we will choose the clubs and the mentoring programmes.
B: Yes, I am entirely satisfied with that.
Part 4 — Extension

Part 4 — Follow-up Discussion

Answer these deeper questions.

1. Should prisons focus more on punishment or on rehabilitation?
I am strongly in favour of rehabilitation. If prisoners learn a trade or gain qualifications, they are far less likely to reoffend when they leave.
2. Is it ever acceptable for ordinary people to break the law?
In exceptional circumstances, yes. Peaceful protest has historically been indispensable in bringing about social change, even when it technically breaks a law.
3. How effective are security cameras at preventing crime?
They are useful for identifying suspects after an incident, but I doubt they prevent much crime on their own, since determined criminals simply avoid being seen.
4. What role should victims play in the justice system?
Victims deserve to be heard and kept informed, although the final decision must remain with the court. Giving victims too much power could undermine a fair trial.
Vocabulary

Vocabulary & Phrases

deter — to discourage someone from doing something, usually by making it seem risky

reoffend — to commit another crime after having been punished for a previous one

rehabilitation — helping a person return to a normal, useful life after prison or illness

sentence — the punishment that a court officially gives to someone found guilty

witness — a person who sees a crime or accident happen and can describe it

Homework

Reading, Listening & Writing

Reading

Read a news article about a real court case. Write a short summary of the facts and say whether you agree with the verdict.

Listening

Listen to a true-crime podcast episode. Note how the presenter introduces the case and which words they use to describe the evidence.

Writing

Write an essay (140–190 words): 'Prison does more harm than good.' What is your opinion?
